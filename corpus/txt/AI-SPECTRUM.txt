Document purpose
This white paper provides general guidelines for building a high-performance Ethernet connectivity fabric that is suitable for training and inferencing AI and Generative AI (GenAI) models.
The paper presents the design challenges and fabric recommendations for supporting clusters of GPU-enabled hosts working cooperatively to process new AI and GenAI workloads. These workloads have moved from the realm of scientific and specialized applications and exploded into the general enterprise market.
Creating an infrastructure for AI and GenAI requires careful consideration. This paper highlights the cluster architecture, with a focus on the unique fabric implementations that can mean the difference between a highly successful cluster and one that fails to deliver results. This architecture begins with an understanding of the dramatic pressure that GenAI training workloads place on the network. These jobs require large numbers of GPUs with extensive memory iteratively sharing model data with all the other GPUs until an optimal solution is achieved.
This process can take days or even weeks, and each iteration moves all the data through the fabric in large, synchronized “elephant flows” that must be completed before the next iteration can begin. Elephant flows are large, long‑lived network traffic streams that consume high bandwidth. Any delay in any of these transfers is known as "tail latency" about the job completion time. Tail latency can result in the entire cluster being kept idle and not performing its function. Further, given that every GPU is talking to every other GPU, the fabric must be able to spread traffic evenly across the fabric and avoid flow collisions.
Finally, any loss of data or need for retransmission can have dramatic consequences for tail latency and should be avoided. Advanced methods for congestion management and lossless data communication techniques address this issue.
This document describes basic networking concepts and approaches for greenfield AI Ethernet fabric deployments using NVIDIA Spectrum with Dell SONiC.
The document also describes the software and hardware components that are required to build a Dell AI environment based on the Dell PowerEdge XE9680 compute node, explaining how the XE9680 node is paired with the Dell PowerSwitch SN-Series 800 GbE and 400 GbE data center product family running the Dell SONiC operating system.
The document is not intended to imply a preference for one fabric deployment approach over another; these approaches have different merits depending on customer requirements. For detailed analysis and in-depth reviews of each approach, consult the documentation that is available from the relevant solution provider.

AI fabric requirements
An AI fabric must be: ● Lossless ● High performance ● Scalable Large Language Models (LLMs) require the exchange of a large volume of data, creating significant network traffic. Characteristics of AI data traffic patterns are: ● High-rate data exchange taking place during the initial training phase of any model ● Latency-sensitive applications exchanging vast amounts of data in a feedback loop fashion ● Predictable but diverse traffic patterns ● Heterogenous traffic size flows (such as elephant flows) ● Chaotic and bursty dataset patterns Fabrics for GenAI compute clusters must deliver the highest bandwidth and lowest latency data transfer while avoiding packet loss or any kind of retransmission delays. Finally, effective utilization of the compute resources depends on minimizing delays that are caused by the network transfers to allow the parallel compute jobs to progress in a synchronized fashion.

Scale-up fabric
GPUs provide not only numerous compute cores, but also their own communication capabilities. For example, an NVIDIA H100 GPU provides more than 10,000 computation CUDA cores and 18 NVLink4 interfaces, each running at 200 Gb/s, an aggregate bandwidth of 450 GB/s. The most advanced NICs today run at 400 Gb/s, while a single H100 GPU can drive a bandwidth of 450 GB/s, more than an order of magnitude higher. Looking ahead, the GB300 (Blackwell Ultra) has 20,480 CUDA cores and 640 Tensor cores, with 288 GB HBM3e memory. The GB300 uses an NVLink 5 with a bidirectional bandwidth of 1.8 TB/s.
As shown in the following figure, the scale-up fabric interconnects the eight GPUs in an XE9680 to form a high-bandwidth domain using the native interfaces of the GPU. This type of fabric enables efficient sharing and coordination of embedded resources, allowing multiple GPUs to work together. Scale-up fabrics can seamlessly address complex tasks such as AI training and fine-tuning and are transparently supported by the communication library that the GPU vendor provides.

Pure Rail topology
The following figure shows the connectivity properties of the Pure Rail topology:

Figure 11. Pure Rail topology
In this topology, each NIC is connected to a different switch (or spine-leaf network) called a rail, as shown. This topology enables direct scale-out fabric communication only between GPUs belonging to the same rail. The scale-up fabric participates in scale-out fabric communications when traffic needs to “cross rails.”
For example, to communicate with GPU 8 on server 2, GPU 4 on server 1 must first transfer its data into the memory of GPU 8 on server 1, known as “crossing the rails.” GPU 8 on server 1 can then communicate through NIC 8 on server 1 with GPU 8 on server 2, which is coupled with NIC 8 on server 2.

Rail Optimized topology
The following figure shows the connectivity properties of the Rail Optimized topology:

Figure 13. Rail Optimized topology
In this topology, each NIC is connected to a different switch (or spine-leaf network), called a rail. The rails are also interconnected at an upper tier, so this topology provides two ways to cross rails—through the scale-up fabric (preferred) or through the upper tier of the scale-out topology.
To communicate with GPU 8 on server 2, GPU 4 on server 1 can:
● Transfer its data into the memory of GPU 8 on server 1 (cross the rails), and then GPU 8 on server 1 can communicate through NIC 8 on server 1 with GPU 8 on server 2, coupled with NIC 8 on server 2.
● Send its data to NIC 4 on server 1, which can reach through the upper tier NIC 8 on server 2, which is connected to GPU 8 on server 2.

At times, the GPU to GPU across different nodes may not take the optimum path (leaf), and the upper tier must provide connectivity between the different rails. If for any reason this data path is not available, the red GPU transmits its data upstream towards Rail1, Rail1 transmits forward towards S1, and S1 sends this data downstream to Rail3.

Frontend fabric
The frontend fabric is the network infrastructure interconnecting the different functions of the AI environment. This fabric facilitates communication, compute resource sharing, and data transfer among the interconnected clusters in the cluster. The frontend fabric also offers end users access to the servers and applications that are a necessary part of the AI environment and supports connectivity for network management through in-band interfaces. When integrated with the storage fabric, the frontend fabric facilitates access to the storage, leading to a comprehensive unified fabric. The bandwidth link speed from the PowerEdge XE9680 node to the frontend fabric is 400 GbE using NICs 9 and 10 (see Figure 8). Those NICs connect to Dell SONiC switches.

Figure 15. Frontend Fabric
The frontend fabric is a converged fabric where storage, application, and in-band GPU cluster management connections take place at speeds ranging between 400 and 25 GbE. The frontend fabric uses EVPN VXLAN and standard Layer 2 or Layer 3 to provide services such as multitenancy access into the AI environment or straight access to storage services from the GPUs as needed. These VLAN segments—storage, GPU application, and in-band cluster management—are then routed within the frontend fabric to reach outside the AI environment or stay isolated as a private AI environment sandbox. In most cases, the connections from the storage, application, and in-band cluster management to the PowerSwitch operate at Layer 2. Each of the different services is assigned a respective VLAN as traffic leaves the devices.
Management network
The management fabric provides out of band (OOB) management connectivity—local or remote—into the AI infrastructure (switches) and into devices such as storage appliances and servers. The OOB network encompasses simple iDRAC connectivity for the servers, as well as management interfaces for the storage appliances and networking devices. The typical link speed of these connections is 1 GbE using Cat-RJ45 copper cables. The following figure shows the OOB connections from all the devices in the Dell AI environment:

State-of-the-art host and GPU connectivity for the industry is based on the RDMA protocol, which can be implemented over Ethernet using RoCE standards. This protocol is supported by high-speed switching, with 800G port speeds as the current benchmark. RoCE is further enhanced by sophisticated load-balancing and congestion-management techniques that ensure lossless, low-latency data transfers to maximize utilization of the compute resources and provide optimal training times and inferencing responses.
AI workloads with NVIDIA Spectrum with Dell SONiC switching options support 800G bandwidth RDMA transmission, and the associated load-balancing and congestion-management techniques that are required to meet GenAI traffic requirements.
